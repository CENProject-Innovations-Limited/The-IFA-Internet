From Ikin to Internet — how four ancient African computers quietly invented the binary idea that still runs every phone, laptop, and network on Earth.

SINGLE MARK = 1 · DOUBLE MARK = 0 · 8 THROWS = ONE FULL BYTE

The Big Idea: One Byte, 256 Odù

Every time a Babalawo casts the sacred palm nuts, it comes down to just two outcomes — a single mark or a double mark, like a coin flip. Flip that "coin" 8 times and you get 256 possible patterns. That's the exact same number of values packed into one byte of computer memory (0–255). Yoruba diviners were running 8-bit binary code — the same math inside your phone — long before the first computer was ever built.

8 binary marks → 256 Odù → 1 byte (0–255)
PART ONE · IMMEMORIAL

Meet the Ancient Computers

Four Yoruba divination tools, redesigned here as the hardware they really were: input, output, memory, and processor — built from nuts, shells, seeds and string.

Ikin Ifá Computer

Ìkin Ifá
The Original Processor

Sixteen sacred palm nuts pass quickly from hand to hand, leaving one or two behind each round. One mark, two marks — a physical coin flip. Do it eight times and a Babalawo builds one full binary sign, one bit at a time.

Input16 palm nuts
Outputsingle/double mark
Cycles8 throws / sign
Memory256 Odù archive
→ becomes the Classical Computer

Ọ̀pẹ̀lẹ̀ Computer

Ọ̀pẹ̀lẹ̀
The Portable Model

Eight seed-halves linked into one chain. Flick it once and all eight faces land at the same time — a whole 8-bit sign computed in a single toss, instead of eight. Small enough to carry anywhere.

Input1 chain, 8 halves
Outputfull sign, one throw
Speedinstant read
Formpocket-sized
→ becomes the Mobile Phone

Ẹ́rìndínlógún Computer

Ẹ́rìndínlógún · "Òòṣà Computer"
The Everyday Network

Sixteen cowrie shells are cast onto a mat and read by how many land mouth-up. It taps into many different Òrìṣà — each one its own knowledge server — making it the most widely used oracle in the community.

Input16 cowrie shells
Outputshells face-up count
Networkmany Òrìṣà "servers"
Userscommunity-wide
→ becomes The Internet

Agbigba Computer

Àgbìgbà
The Parallel Processor

Instead of one string, Agbigba uses several cords at once, each strung with four markers. Throw them together and you don't get one answer — you get four Odù at the same time, computed side by side in a single motion.

Inputmultiple 4-mark cords
Output4 Odù per throw
Processingparallel & simultaneous
Styledirect & fast
→ becomes the Quantum Computer
same binary idea · millenia later
PART TWO · TODAY

Meet Their Modern Descendants

Same binary logic, new hardware. Here's where each ancient computer's big idea ended up.

Classical Computer

Inspired by Ìkin Ifá

Every laptop and desktop runs on the idea a Babalawo used first: break every answer into two states, 1 or 0, and combine enough of them to represent words, pictures, games — even this page.

Logicbinary, 1s & 0s
Unit8 bits = 1 byte
Storagebillions of bytes
Jobone step at a time

Mobile Phone

Inspired by Ọ̀pẹ̀lẹ̀

Just like the Ọ̀pẹ̀lẹ̀'s single flick, your phone is built for speed you carry anywhere — one tap computes an answer instantly, wherever you are.

Inputtouch, voice, camera
Outputinstant answer
Formpocket-sized
Alwaysin your hand

The Internet

Inspired by Ẹ́rìndínlógún

The Internet connects billions of devices the same way Ẹ́rìndínlógún connects many Òrìṣà — one question travels through countless servers worldwide before an answer comes back to you.

Nodesbillions of devices
Networkworldwide
Userseveryone, everywhere
Jobcarry the message

Quantum Computer

Inspired by Agbigba

Where a classical computer checks answers one at a time, a quantum computer — like Agbigba's many cords cast together — explores many possibilities at once, tackling some problems dramatically faster.

Unitqubits, not just bits
Stylemany states at once
Statusstill being invented
Best athuge, complex problems
