Production · Series 1 of 16 · Ìká Méjì

Set in a Yoruba kingdom that has made mastery itself a law — ỌLÁBERINJỌ, a scholar of extraordinary range, arrives at the compound of the 17th house summoned by a king who has outlawed the half-knowing. Over four acts, through the 16 disciplines of the kingdom and the weight of a fallen master's name, she must decide whether knowledge is a thing one possesses or a thing one becomes.

Ìká Méjì · Part One

Opening Passage · Chapter One · Ìbẹ̀rẹ̀

The compound of the seventeenth house was not the largest in the kingdom. It was not the highest. What it was — what every person who arrived there felt before they could name it — was correct. As if the ground had been placed exactly where ground should be. As if the walls had been built not to enclose but to define the particular quality of the air inside them.

Ọláberinjọ stood at the outer gate and understood, for the first time in her life, that she had been afraid of this moment. Not of the king. Of the question she would be asked, which she already knew, and which she did not yet know the answer to.

She had mastered eleven of the sixteen disciplines. The other five she had touched. Touching is not knowing. She understood this now, standing at the gate of the one man in the kingdom for whom the difference was not academic.
Ká ni ká. Ìmọ̀ ni o.
To master is to master. Knowledge is it.

Ká
To Master
To know a thing so completely that the thing and the knower are no longer distinguishable. The seeker does not possess knowledge — the seeker becomes it. This is the law of the 17th compound.
Kótodé
The Transient
One who passes through knowledge without dwelling long enough to know it. The civilizational crisis at the center of ÒDÈ-ÌKÁ. We learn in borrowed languages and borrowed frames. We pass. We do not dwell.
Ẹnu Awo
The Diviner's Mouth
The diviner's mouth as instrument — what is spoken carries the force of an axe. Ààke ní ń'bagi sá: the axe hits and runs back to the one who threw it. To name a thing correctly is to be changed by it.

The People of Ìká Méjì

Series 1 · Part One Characters

Ọ
Ọláberinjọ
The Seeker · Protagonist
A young scholar of extraordinary range — eleven of sixteen disciplines mastered, five touched. She arrives at the 17th compound carrying nothing, which is the first correct thing about her. The question the king will ask, she already knows. The answer, she does not yet have.
Ẹni tí a kò lè kọ ní ìrọ̀ tọ̀run rẹ̀.
O
Oníkàámògún
King of the 17th · The Law
The king who outlawed the half-knowing. Oní-ìká-àámògún — the one whose name contains the Odu's law. He does not rule by force but by the sheer precision of his understanding. He plays Ayò Ọlọ́pọ́nfá the way other kings hold court. His words are not declarations — they are Ẹnu Awo.
Ìjà wa ni: a jẹ kótodé. A ń kọjá. A ò ń gbé.
L
Lákáa
The Strong Snail · The Fallen
A master who died not because someone surpassed him, but because no one was strong enough to meet him equally. Lákáa jẹ́ ìgbín kakaka. A force requires a corresponding force, or it has nowhere to go. He is the lesson the kingdom has not yet finished learning.
Lákáa jẹ́ ìgbín kakaka. Ó lagbára. Ohun tí ó pa á kò jẹ́ ẹni alágbára.
P
Pàràká Kinní
First Ancestral Witness · Egungun
A small Egungun masquerade — pàràká. Not a parrot. Stands motionless and masked. Speaks in the compressed authority of the ancestors: terse, final, unrepeatable. When the pàràká says a word three times, the word becomes a reality. The masquerade is not wearing the ancestor. It is the ancestor.
Ká — to master. To know a thing so completely that knower and known are indistinguishable.
P
Pàràká Kejì
Second Ancestral Witness · Egungun
The second small Egungun masquerade. Affirms in the ancestral voice. When the Ẹnu Awo is complete, the two pàràká are absolutely still. The ancestral witnesses have heard. What was spoken will move.
Ààke. The word lands like the thing itself.
Ì
Ìyálóde Ṣàngọ̀ẹkò
Market Queen · The Counter-Question
The market queen from the Alákàrà quarter. Not a scholar. The woman who trades in the market everyone passes through without stopping. She asks the one question the Great Hall needs to hear — and the king's answer inverts everything: the scholars must learn the market-woman's knowledge, not the other way.
Ọba, àwa olójà — ṣé ẹ ní kí a mọ ìmọ̀ àwọn ọ̀mọ̀wé?
